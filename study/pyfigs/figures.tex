\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig01_coupling}
  \caption{Coupling between navigation quality and manoeuvre cost is
  conditional on the object's ephemeris. (a)~Mean tangential impulse over $1500$
  sampled conjunction geometries against the navigation uncertainty at the
  encounter, one curve per object ephemeris uncertainty
  $\sigma_{\mathrm{obj}}$; the shaded band is the range spanned by the measured
  duty-cycled GNSS law of Fig.~\ref{fig:nav} ($4.8$--$32.1$\,m). (b)~The same
  data as a sensitivity: the increase in mean $\Delta v$ across that band.
  Tightening the navigation only buys something when the object is known better
  than the band itself, $+62\,\%$ at $\sigma_{\mathrm{obj}} = 10$\,m against
  $+0.5\,\%$ at $300$\,m, a factor of $128$. The mean is used rather than the
  median because at $\sigma_{\mathrm{obj}} = 10$\,m and
  $\sigma_{\mathrm{nav}} = 4.8$\,m, $49.7\,\%$ of the geometries need no
  manoeuvre at all, so the median sits on an atom of probability mass at zero and
  any ratio taken through it is unstable.}
  \label{fig:coupling}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig02_koz_consistency}
  \caption{The inflated keep-out radius must be a function of time, not of the
  prediction mesh. Adding a fixed per-step process-noise increment makes the
  radius depend on how the horizon is split; discretising the continuous
  process-noise power spectral density over the step with the van Loan method
  removes that dependence, and the three splittings collapse onto one curve.
  (a)~In the flight configuration the $\sigma$ cap binds after about $90$\,s and
  saturates both formulations at $249.7$\,m, so the defect is visible only
  earlier: $17.6$\,m of mesh spread at $t = 60$\,s. (b)~With the cap removed the
  formulation is seen bare: the per-step scheme spreads $619$\,m across the three
  meshes and under-inflates the converged radius by a factor of $2$ to $3$. The
  cap that hides the defect in flight is itself the auxiliary-sensor assumption
  of Fig.~\ref{fig:nav}, not a property of the guidance law.}
  \label{fig:koz}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig03_architecture_cost}
  \caption{A keep-out constraint sampled on the guidance grid does not see the
  conjunction. (a)~At the relative velocities of a real plane-crossing encounter
  the object stays inside the inflated keep-out sphere for $2d/v_{\mathrm{rel}}$,
  a median of $34$\,ms over the $1500$ sampled geometries, while the guidance
  grid steps every $h$ seconds: the deployed $30$\,s step is $886$ times longer
  than the window it is meant to detect. (b)~With the encounter epoch uniform
  with respect to the grid, the chance that any sample falls inside is
  $\min(1,\,2d/(v_{\mathrm{rel}}h))$: $0.11\,\%$ at the deployed step and still
  only $1.1\,\%$ at $h = 3$\,s. Shrinking the step does not fix it, because the
  probability grows only as $1/h$. The constraint must therefore be imposed where
  the minimum occurs, by solving for the time of closest approach continuously,
  which is what the proposed guidance layer does.}
  \label{fig:blind}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig05_navigation}
  \caption{What duty-cycling the GNSS receiver does and does not buy.
  (a)~Navigation uncertainty against time since the last fix: measured over a
  $4000$\,s run, reproduced by the filter's covariance recursion, and compared
  with the level reached when the receiver is never switched on. The three
  coincide, because the $32.1$\,m ceiling is the Riccati fixed point of an
  auxiliary measurement channel that is always enabled in the model
  ($100$\,m per axis on position plus $50$\,m on altitude), not a consequence of
  the duty cycle. (b)~Sweeping the duty cycle confirms it: the ceiling is flat
  across the whole range while the time-averaged uncertainty falls from $32.1$ to
  $3.7$\,m. What the receiver buys is the mean, $24.3$\,m at the $19.45\,\%$ duty
  cycle flown, and not the bound. The bound follows the assumed auxiliary sensor
  roughly as its square root: at $300$\,m per axis it would be $72.9$\,m.}
  \label{fig:nav}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig08_probability_dilution}
  \caption{Why the design constrains a distance and not a collision
  probability. (a)~With the miss distance held at $120$\,m, the collision
  probability computed with the Foster formulation \emph{falls} from
  $8.8\times10^{-4}$ to $1.6\times10^{-4}$ as the navigation uncertainty grows by
  a factor of $21$: a larger covariance spreads the same probability mass over a
  larger area. A guidance law triggered on a $P_c$ threshold would therefore
  reward worse navigation. (b)~The covariance-inflated keep-out distance instead
  grows monotonically, $333 \rightarrow 835$\,m, and with it the required
  impulse. This sweep is the standalone conjunction demonstrator, with its own
  object-covariance assumption of $\sigma_{\mathrm{obj}} = 150$\,m; its impulses
  are an upper bound, because that demonstrator searches only the posigrade
  branch, which costs $13$ to $38\,\%$ more than the retrograde root the flight
  planner selects. The comparison rests on the monotonicity, which is unaffected.}
  \label{fig:dilution}
\end{figure}
